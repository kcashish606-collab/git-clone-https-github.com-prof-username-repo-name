# Script — What If You Fell Into the Mariana Trench?

**Format:** "What if" depth journey (the deep sea / scary nature pillar, same family as the channel's latest upload "Scientists Found 31 New Terrifying Creatures in the Atlantic")
**Target length:** about 7:05 (about 1,010 narrated words at about 140 wpm). To pass 8:00 for mid-roll ads, add 1–2 more creatures (giant squid, vampire squid) in the twilight and midnight zones.
**Device:** a depth meter in the corner of the screen the whole time. It's the retention engine: viewers stay to see the number at the bottom.

---

### COLD OPEN / HOOK
**[VISUAL: Bright blue sea surface, a cartoon character in a transparent glass bubble floating. Depth meter in the corner: 0 m.]**

NARRATOR:
Imagine you're floating in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, inside a glass bubble strong enough to survive anything. Below you is the deepest place on Earth. It's so deep that if you dropped Mount Everest into it, its peak would still be more than **2 kilometers underwater**.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon Everest sinking into the trench, water closing over the top.]**

On the way down, you'll pass a sunken legend, see fish with see-through heads, and meet creatures that make their own light. And at the very bottom, you'll find something nobody expected to see down there.

Ready? Let's drop.

---

### 0 M — WHERE ARE WE?
**[VISUAL: Globe spinning to the western Pacific, near Guam.]**

The Mariana Trench is in the western Pacific Ocean, near the island of Guam. It's about 2,500 kilometers long, a giant crescent-shaped scar in the ocean floor. And its deepest point, called the **Challenger Deep**, is nearly **11,000 meters** down.

**[ON SCREEN: "CHALLENGER DEEP: ~10,935 m"]**

But first, what if you skipped the bubble and just… jumped in?

---

### 10 M — YOUR BODY STARTS TO SQUEEZE
**[VISUAL: Character without bubble, swimming down, cheeks puffing, ears with little pain stars.]**

Every 10 meters you go down, the water adds another full atmosphere of pressure on you. At just 10 meters, the pressure is already **double** what you feel on land. The air in your lungs gets squeezed to half its size. Your ears start to hurt.

At around 30 meters, divers breathing compressed air can feel **nitrogen narcosis**. It's a weird, dizzy, almost drunk feeling that divers call "the rapture of the deep."

**[VISUAL: Character giggling at a fish. Fish looks concerned.]**

Recreational scuba divers usually stop at about **40 meters**. The deepest a free diver has gone on a single breath is a little over **200 meters**. Without serious equipment, humans just aren't built for this.

So, back in the bubble you go.

**[VISUAL: Character zipped back into the glass bubble. Thumbs up.]**

---

### 200 M — THE LIGHTS GO OUT
**[VISUAL: Water color fading from bright blue to deep navy. Depth meter: 200 m.]**

As you sink, colors disappear one by one. Red goes first, in just the first few meters, then orange, then yellow. By 200 meters, you've left the **sunlight zone** and entered the **twilight zone**. There's barely enough light down here to tell up from down.

And this is where things get weird.

**[VISUAL: Barreleye fish with transparent dome head, glowing green eyes looking upward.]**

Meet the **barreleye fish**. It has a completely see-through head, and its glowing green eyes sit *inside* it, looking straight up to spot the shadows of prey above.

---

### 1,000 M — TOTAL DARKNESS
**[VISUAL: Pitch black. Then little sparkles of blue light pop up all around the bubble.]**

At 1,000 meters, sunlight is completely gone. Welcome to the **midnight zone**. The water is just a few degrees above freezing.

But it's not totally dark. Look around. Many deep-sea animals make their *own* light. It's called **bioluminescence**, and in one study, scientists found that about **three out of four** deep-sea animals they observed could glow.

**[VISUAL: Anglerfish appears, glowing lure dangling, huge needle-teeth grin. Bubble character screams silently.]**

And here's the most famous resident: the **anglerfish**. She dangles a glowing "fishing rod" in front of her mouth to lure in dinner.

Oh, and by the way, in some species, the male is tiny. He bites onto the female and slowly *fuses* with her body, permanently. Talk about a clingy relationship.

---

### 3,800 M — A SUNKEN LEGEND
**[VISUAL: The bow of a ghostly cartoon ship emerges from the darkness.]**

At about 3,800 meters, you pass the depth where the **Titanic** lies. Here it is: the most famous shipwreck in the world, sitting in total darkness in the North Atlantic. It's far from here, but at exactly this depth.

**[ON SCREEN: "TITANIC DEPTH: ~3,800 m"]**

And you're still only about a third of the way down.

But that's nothing compared to what happens next.

---

### 4,000–6,000 M — THE ABYSS
**[VISUAL: Wide, flat seafloor plain dotted with strange creatures.]**

Now you're in the **abyssal zone**. The pressure here could crush a soda can like paper. Food is so rare that most animals survive on "marine snow," tiny bits of dead plants and animals slowly drifting down from above.

**[VISUAL: Dumbo octopus flapping its ear-like fins, waving at the bubble.]**

Say hi to the **dumbo octopus**, named after the flying elephant because of its ear-like fins. It's one of the deepest-living octopuses known.

---

### 6,000 M — THE HADAL ZONE
**[VISUAL: Walls of the trench rising on both sides. Ominous music.]**

Below 6,000 meters, you enter a part of the ocean so extreme that scientists named it after the Greek god of the underworld, Hades. This is the **hadal zone**, and it only exists inside deep trenches.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon Hades waving from a trench wall.]**

At around 8,000 meters, you meet a small, pale, jelly-like fish: the **Mariana snailfish**. Its relatives hold the record for the deepest fish ever filmed, at about 8,300 meters, in a trench nearby. Scientists think fish probably can't survive much deeper than this. Their bodies simply can't handle more pressure.

**[ON SCREEN: "DEEPEST FISH EVER FILMED: ~8,300 m (nearby trench)"]**

So from here on down… no more fish.

---

### 10,935 M — THE BOTTOM
**[VISUAL: Bubble lands softly on gray silt, a little puff of sediment.]**

Thump. You've made it. The bottom of the Challenger Deep.

The pressure here is more than **1,000 times** what you feel at the surface. That's like having around 8 tons pressing on every square inch of your body. The temperature is just a few degrees above freezing.

And yet… there's life. Little shrimp-like creatures called **amphipods** scuttle across the mud. And giant single-celled organisms called **xenophyophores**, some as big as your hand, sit on the seafloor.

**[VISUAL: Amphipods crowding the bubble curiously.]**

But here's the thing nobody wanted to find.

**[VISUAL: A plastic bag drifting into view. Music stops.]**

In 2018, scientists reported spotting a **plastic bag** almost 11 kilometers down. The deepest place on Earth, and our trash got there first.

---

### WHO HAS REALLY BEEN HERE?
**[VISUAL: Retro filter, the bathyscaphe Trieste sinking, two cartoon explorers inside.]**

The first people to reach the bottom were Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh in **1960**, in a submersible called the *Trieste*. The trip down took almost 5 hours, and on the way, one of the windows cracked. They stayed at the bottom for only about 20 minutes.

Then nobody went back for over 50 years, until filmmaker **James Cameron** made a solo dive in **2012**. Since then, a few more explorers have made the trip.

**[ON SCREEN: "More people have been to SPACE than to the Challenger Deep"]**

In fact, far more people have traveled to space than have ever reached this spot.

---

### OUTRO / CTA
**[VISUAL: Bubble rising toward a bright sunlit surface; depth meter rolling back to 0.]**

So, would you take the trip? Tell me in the comments what depth *you'd* turn back at. I'll go first: I'm out the moment I see that anglerfish.

If you learned something new, hit like, share this with someone who loves the ocean (or is terrified of it), and subscribe for more. And remember… stay on the bright side of life!

**[END SCREEN]**

## Fact-check list (verify before recording)
- Challenger Deep depth: estimates range about 10,900–10,935 m
- Everest 8,849 m. About 2 km of water above it if sunk at Challenger Deep
- +1 atm per 10 m; pressure at bottom >1,000 atm (~16,000 psi ≈ 8 US tons per sq inch)
- Recreational limit 40 m; no-limits freediving record 214 m (Herbert Nitsch, 2007)
- Bioluminescence: Martini & Haddock (2017), about 76% of observed animals in Monterey Bay
- Titanic wreck about 3,800 m
- Deepest fish filmed about 8,336 m (snailfish, Izu-Ogasawara Trench, 2022, reported 2023). Note: filmed in a neighboring trench, not the Mariana
- Plastic bag at about 10,898 m (Chiba et al., 2018, Marine Policy)
- Trieste 1960 (Piccard & Walsh): about 4 h 47 min descent, about 20 min at bottom, cracked window
- Cameron solo dive 2012
