# Script — Why Planes Almost Never Fly Over Tibet

**Format:** "Hidden reason" explainer (the "Why planes never fly over…" pillar)
**Target length:** about 7:05 (about 1,030 narrated words at about 140 wpm). To pass 8:00 for mid-roll ads, add 2–3 more places to the BONUS list.
**Tone:** friendly narrator, second person, a pattern interrupt every 20–40 seconds

---

### COLD OPEN / HOOK
**[VISUAL: Cartoon passenger asleep by an airplane window. Flight map on the seat screen shows a curved route bending around a huge empty area.]**

NARRATOR:
Next time you fly between Europe and Asia, look at the flight map. You might notice something strange. Your plane bends around a huge chunk of the planet, like it's avoiding something.

**[VISUAL: Map zooms in; the empty area is labeled "TIBET".]**

That "something" is the Tibetan Plateau. It's bigger than Western Europe and called the Roof of the World. Most airlines try very hard not to fly over it.

It's not a secret base. It's not a curse. The real reason is about what would happen in the first 12 minutes if something went wrong up there. And it's scarier than any conspiracy theory.

---

### THE ROOF OF THE WORLD
**[VISUAL: 3D cartoon map rising up; a tiny cartoon climber gasping for air.]**

First, let's see how high we're talking.

The Tibetan Plateau has an average elevation of more than **4,500 meters**, about 14,800 feet. That's the average. Its *ground level* is almost as high as the top of Mont Blanc, the highest mountain in Western Europe.

And sticking out of it are the Himalayas, with dozens of peaks above 7,000 meters, including Mount Everest at **8,849 meters**.

**[ON SCREEN: "AVERAGE GROUND HEIGHT: 4,500+ m"]**

Now, a passenger jet usually cruises at around 10,000 to 12,000 meters. So over Tibet, there's a lot less empty air between you and the ground than you'd think.

Okay, so what? Planes clear the mountains just fine. And that's true… as long as everything goes perfectly.

---

### PROBLEM #1 — THE 12-MINUTE PROBLEM
**[VISUAL: Cabin interior, everyone calm. Then a loud "BANG," fog fills the cabin, yellow oxygen masks drop.]**

Imagine you're cruising along and suddenly the cabin loses pressure. Maybe a crack in a window or a door seal failure. It's rare, but it happens.

At cruising altitude, the air outside is so thin that you could lose consciousness in **as little as 30 seconds to a minute**. That's why those yellow masks drop from the ceiling.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon passenger putting on mask, then helping kid. ON SCREEN: "Put YOUR mask on first!"]**

But here's what most people don't know. On many planes, those masks get their oxygen from a chemical generator that only lasts about **12 to 20 minutes**.

That's not a design flaw. It's on purpose. The masks only need to keep you going long enough for the pilots to dive down to about **3,000 meters**, roughly 10,000 feet, where you can breathe normally again.

**[VISUAL: Plane diving down over flat ocean, passengers relieved.]**

Over the ocean or most countries, no problem. Dive, breathe, land.

But over Tibet?

**[VISUAL: Plane tries to dive, a giant mountain wall appears below. Record-scratch sound.]**

The *ground* is already higher than 3,000 meters. There's nowhere to dive to. The pilots would have to keep flying high, through thin air, until they get off the plateau. And that could take longer than the oxygen lasts.

---

### PROBLEM #2 — WHEN AN ENGINE QUITS
**[VISUAL: Plane with one engine sputtering, little cartoon smoke puff.]**

Most long-haul planes today have just two engines. And they can fly perfectly well on one. But not as high.

When one engine fails, a plane can't hold its cruising altitude. It slowly sinks to a lower height. Pilots call this "drift down."

Over flat land, that's fine. Over the Himalayas, "slowly sinking" toward mountains that are 7 or 8 kilometers tall is a very different story.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon pilot sweating, looking at a mountain getting closer through the windshield.]**

That's why airlines that do fly near this region plan special escape routes in advance: exact paths to follow down valleys and away from the highest peaks if something goes wrong.

---

### PROBLEM #3 — NOWHERE TO LAND
**[VISUAL: Map of Tibet with only a few tiny airport icons scattered across a huge empty space.]**

Let's say the pilots need to land *right now*. Over most of Europe or the US, there's usually an airport within a short flight.

Over Tibet? Airports are few and far between. And the ones that exist are some of the highest in the world. Lhasa's airport sits at about 3,570 meters. And Daocheng Yading Airport, at about 4,400 meters, is often called the highest civilian airport on Earth.

**[ON SCREEN: "DAOCHENG YADING AIRPORT: ~4,411 m"]**

At that height, the air is so thin that engines produce less power and wings produce less lift. Planes need longer runways to take off and land, and pilots need special training to fly there.

So an emergency landing on the plateau isn't exactly a relaxing option.

But that's nothing compared to the invisible danger.

---

### PROBLEM #4 — INVISIBLE TURBULENCE
**[VISUAL: Calm blue sky, then invisible wavy lines appear around the plane, which suddenly jolts. Coffee flies.]**

When strong winds slam into huge mountains, they don't just go over them. They bounce, ripple and form giant invisible waves in the sky, called **mountain waves**.

Add the jet stream, a fast river of air high in the atmosphere, and you get something pilots really dislike: **clear-air turbulence**. It can hit without any clouds or warning on the radar.

**[VISUAL: Cartoon flight attendant clinging to the cart. Cartoon passenger: "I KNEW I should've kept my seatbelt on!"]**

Oh, and by the way, this is why pilots always ask you to keep your seatbelt fastened, even when the sign is off.

---

### THE TWIST — IT'S NOT ACTUALLY BANNED
**[VISUAL: Big stamp "NOT ILLEGAL!"]**

Here's the surprising part. Flying over Tibet isn't forbidden. Some planes do it every day. Flights to Lhasa, for example, land right in the middle of the plateau.

But airlines that fly there need extra planning, specially trained pilots, escape routes and sometimes extra oxygen on board. For most long flights, it's simply easier, safer and often cheaper to go around.

So it's not a "no-fly zone." It's more like a "why-would-you-fly zone."

---

### BONUS — PLACES PLANES REALLY CAN'T FLY OVER
**[VISUAL: Quick montage, each place with a red "NO PLANES" badge.]**

But some places really are off-limits.

**Disney World and Disneyland** in the US have restricted airspace above them, so low-flying planes, like those dragging advertising banners, have to stay away.

**[VISUAL: Castle with a sad little banner plane turning away.]**

**Machu Picchu** in Peru: flights over the ancient city are banned to protect the ruins and the wildlife.

**The Taj Mahal** in India is a no-fly zone, partly for security and partly to protect the famous white marble.

And **Area 51** in Nevada has restricted airspace so strict that pilots nicknamed it "the Box."

**[VISUAL: Alien peeking out of a hangar, quickly shutting the door.]**

---

### OUTRO / CTA
**[VISUAL: Plane happily curving around the mountains at sunset.]**

So next time your flight takes the "long way," don't be annoyed. Your pilots might be keeping you far away from a roof you really don't want to land on.

Now tell me in the comments: what's the weirdest thing that's ever happened to you on a flight? I read them all!

If you learned something new today, hit like, share this with a friend who's afraid of flying (or maybe don't), and subscribe for more. And remember… stay on the bright side of life!

**[END SCREEN]**
